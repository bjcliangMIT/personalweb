# Research Statement

Chen Liang

My research examines how local interactions among individuals shape collective outcomes in organizations, markets, and digitally mediated communities. I study settings in which coordination is difficult, incentives are only partially aligned, and formal rules interact with emergent social practices. Across these settings, a common question motivates my work: how do micro-level patterns of attention, interpretation, and behavior accumulate into durable organization-level outcomes such as polarization, governance capacity, and strategic conduct? I approach this question by combining computational social science methods, including agent-based modeling, system dynamics, network analysis, and natural language processing, with qualitative methods such as interviews and participatory observation. This mixed-method approach allows me to develop theory about complex social processes while staying close to the empirical realities of organizational life.

My research agenda sits at the intersection of organization theory, strategy, and economic sociology. I am especially interested in contexts where organizations must govern behavior that they do not fully control, including digital platforms, groups facing ideological conflict, and boards making consequential strategic decisions. In each of these domains, outcomes depend not only on formal structure or incentives, but also on how people respond to one another over time. By tracing these dynamics, I aim to contribute to scholarship on organizations and strategy while also offering tools for understanding contemporary challenges in governance and collective action.

My job market paper, "Data-Making Fans and Platform Governance" with Vicky Chuqiao Yang, examines how fan labor, informal data practices, and platform governance co-evolve in digitally mediated communities. Digital platforms rely extensively on user activity, yet users do more than simply consume or create content. In many communities, they also generate classification systems, monitoring practices, and informal infrastructures that make coordination possible. This project asks how these user-generated practices shape platform governance and, in turn, how platform interventions reshape the communities that sustain them. Drawing on qualitative evidence and a process-oriented theoretical lens, we show that platform governance is not merely imposed from above; it is partially constituted through the distributed, often unpaid efforts of community participants. The project contributes to research on platform strategy and governance by showing how organizations adapt to and depend upon forms of labor and knowledge production that emerge outside formal boundaries. More broadly, it highlights how governance capacity can arise from the interaction between organizational authority and collective user practices.

My second stream of research studies political polarization and collective action. In my candidacy paper, "From Opinion Dynamics to Collective Action," I examine how asymmetric tolerance can transform disagreement into durable polarization. Much existing work on polarization focuses either on static differences in preferences or on macro-level outcomes such as sorting and conflict. My approach instead models the interactional mechanisms that link everyday exposure, influence, and boundary-setting to larger collective outcomes. Using computational modeling, I show how small asymmetries in willingness to engage across difference can generate persistent divides and alter the conditions under which collective action becomes possible. This work contributes to organization theory and economic sociology by clarifying how micro-level social processes can produce stable patterns of fragmentation even in the absence of strong formal segmentation. It also speaks to broader questions about coalition formation, social movements, and the governance of heterogeneous groups.

My third stream of work considers strategic behavior in elite decision-making settings. In "From Composition to Conduct in the Boardroom," joint work with INADA Takahiro, I use a dynamic NK model to analyze board behavior and decision-making. Research on boards often emphasizes composition, such as independence, expertise, or diversity, but the connection between who sits on a board and how the board actually deliberates remains undertheorized. Our project addresses this gap by modeling board conduct as a dynamic process shaped by interdependence, search, and interaction. By bringing simulation methods to classic questions of board composition and strategic oversight, this work contributes to conversations in strategy and organization theory about how group structure affects decision quality and adaptability. More generally, it extends my broader interest in how collective outcomes emerge from interaction under conditions of complexity.

Although these projects focus on different empirical settings, they are united by a common theoretical commitment: organizations are sustained and transformed through relational processes that cannot be understood solely through static attributes. Whether I study fans coordinating around platform infrastructures, citizens responding to ideological disagreement, or directors navigating strategic tradeoffs, I treat social systems as dynamic and path dependent. This perspective leads me to ask how governance emerges, when it breaks down, and how organizations can remain adaptive in the face of distributed action. I see this as a productive bridge between organization theory and strategy. Organization theory helps explain the social mechanisms through which coordination occurs, while strategy foregrounds the consequences of those mechanisms for adaptation, control, and performance.

Methodologically, I aim to build theory with tools that are well matched to complex social processes. Computational modeling allows me to formalize mechanisms, explore nonlinear effects, and identify conditions under which particular organizational outcomes are likely to emerge. Qualitative work allows me to uncover practices, meanings, and forms of situated expertise that are difficult to capture through abstract models alone. Rather than treating these approaches as substitutes, I use them as complements. My goal is to produce research that is theoretically ambitious, methodologically plural, and empirically grounded. I am particularly excited by opportunities to bring computational methods into closer conversation with core questions in management and sociology, especially where traditional approaches have struggled to capture feedback loops, emergence, and temporal dynamics.

Looking ahead, I plan to extend this research agenda in three directions. First, I will continue studying platform governance by examining how organizations manage user-generated infrastructures, moderation systems, and informal expertise across different digital environments. This line of work will address how firms learn from, incorporate, or constrain distributed contributors, and what these processes imply for organizational boundaries and control. Second, I will deepen my work on polarization and collective behavior by studying how interaction rules, network structures, and institutional interventions affect the possibility of cooperation across difference. This research has implications not only for politics, but also for organizations managing internal disagreement and coalition-building. Third, I will expand my work on strategic decision-making groups, including boards and other elite teams, to better understand how composition, process, and governance design jointly shape organizational adaptation under uncertainty.

Taken together, my research seeks to explain how organizations govern complex social systems when outcomes depend on many interdependent actors. I am motivated by substantive problems that matter both theoretically and practically: how platforms govern communities they rely upon but do not fully control, how disagreement becomes polarization, and how strategic groups make decisions under complexity. By combining formal models with close attention to lived organizational processes, I hope to contribute research that is analytically rigorous, cross-disciplinary, and relevant to some of the most pressing questions in contemporary organizational life.
